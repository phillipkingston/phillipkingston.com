Clan Maclaine of Lochbuie & Clan Ross

Two Highland descents: the Macleans of Mull, from whom the Maclaines of Lochbuie and the MacFadzean sept come, and the earls of Ross, whose Gaelic name the Andersons carry.

Overview

Two clans meet in this family. The Maclaines of Lochbuie hold the southern end of Mull from the fourteenth century, and the MacFadzeans, whose name is also written MacFadyen and McFadyen, were on that ground before them. Clan Ross takes its Gaelic name, Clann Anndrais, from Andrew, which is the same name the Andersons carry. What follows is the documented outline of both, with every claim sourced and the weak links marked as weak.

01

Origins

Gillean of the Battle Axe

The Macleans take their name from Gillean of the Battle Axe, Gilleain na Tuaighe, a thirteenth-century warrior. Clan tradition puts him and his sons at the Battle of Largs in 1263, where Alexander III turned back the Norwegian fleet.1 The clan society traces the line back rather more than seven hundred years from him.5 The Largs story is tradition rather than record, and is best read that way.

Two brothers, two charters

Gillean's great-grandson Iain Dubh, Black John, had two sons: Eachann Reaganach, Hector the Stern, and Lachlan Lubanach, Lachlan the Wily. Each took a separate charter of lands on Mull from the Lord of the Isles around 1350, Hector at Lochbuie and Lachlan at Duart.14 That division is the origin of the two clans, and of the long argument about which is the elder.

Fearchar and the earldom of Ross

Clan Ross begins further north and rather earlier. Fearchar mac an t-Sagairt, son of the priest, came of the hereditary abbots of Applecross and of the Ó Beólláin kindred.37 He put down a rebellion against Alexander II, brought the king the heads of its leaders, and was knighted in 1215; by 1234 he held the title Earl of Ross.3

02

Maclaine of Lochbuie

Moy Castle and the barony

Hector Reaganach built Moy Castle, a tower house at the head of Loch Buie, in the fourteenth century. It stayed the chiefs' residence for four hundred years, until Lochbuie House replaced it in 1752.1 The lands were erected into the barony of Moy in 1542, under the sixth chief, which is why the chief is styled Baron of Moy to this day.15

Lochbuie and Duart

Both houses sat on the Council of the Lord of the Isles.5 Whether Lochbuie or Duart is the senior line has been disputed for centuries and is not settled here. The spelling divides them in modern use: Maclaine for Lochbuie, Maclean for Duart, though the distinction is later than the split itself.

An Irish origin story

The clan also carries a tradition of descent from Maurice Fitzgerald, the Anglo-Norman who came to Ireland with Strongbow, with offshoots said to have reached Scotland in the thirteenth century.1 Origin legends of this kind attach to many Highland clans and are generally later constructions; it is recorded here as tradition, not as descent that can be traced.

03

The MacFadyen Sept

There first

The MacFadyens held the ground at the head of Loch Buie before the Macleans arrived. When Hector Reaganach took his charter around 1350, the land was occupied by them.6 The sept did not join the clan so much as the clan arrived on top of the sept, which is the more usual Highland pattern and the less flattering one.

The arrow and the bone

Tradition tells it sharply. The MacFadyen chief gave Hector leave to build a fortalice at the head of the loch for mutual protection. When it was finished Hector climbed to the top and put an arrow through the bone the MacFadyen chief was eating on the ground below, and the MacFadyen chief, being no fool, removed himself.6 It is a good story and it should be read as one: it explains a transfer of land in the idiom of a duel.

MacFadzean, MacFadyen, McFadyen

The chief's own society lists the sept as MacFadzean.5 The z is not a z: it stands for the older letter yogh, which sounded like a y, so MacFadzean and MacFadyen are one name written two ways, and McFadyen is a third. Whatever the confrontational beginning, the two families became allies, and the MacFadyens are now counted a sept of Maclaine of Lochbuie.6

04

Clan Ross

From Applecross to the earldom

Fearchar's family were the hereditary abbots of Applecross, a lay abbacy of the old Celtic church, and of the Ó Beólláin kindred out of Ireland.37 His name, son of the priest, records that inheritance. The earldom he was granted in the 1220s and 1230s made a provincial lordship out of a church office.3

The line of earls

Fearchar was succeeded around 1251 by his son Uilleam, who appears as early as 1232 witnessing a charter as the son of Ferquhard, Earl of Ross.32 The earldom ran through the Ó Beólláin line for several generations before passing out of the family.

The clan today

The seat is Balnagown Castle in Easter Ross. The chiefship passed in 2024, on the death of David Campbell Ross, to Fiona Campbell Ross of Ross and Balnagowan, recognised by the Lord Lyon King of Arms.2

05

The Anderson Sept

The same name, twice

Clann Anndrais the Gaelic name of Clan Ross: children of Andrew

Anderson is not an outside name attached to Clan Ross by a Victorian list. Clan Ross's own Gaelic name is Clann Anndrais, the children of Andrew.2 Anderson means son of Andrew; the Gaelic Gille Aindreas, servant or devotee of Andrew, gives Gillanders and MacGillanders. The clan and the sept are the same word in different languages.

Gillanders and the early Rosses

Gillanders is frequently found among the early Rosses and is often treated as equivalent to Ross itself, in the line descending from Fearchar mac an t-Sagairt.2 The related form MacAndrew connects the name both to Ross and, from the fifteenth century, to the Clan Chattan federation, so a MacAndrew descent does not by itself settle which clan is meant.

A caution about a common name

Andrew is the patron saint of Scotland, so sons of Andrew appear independently in every part of the country. The name alone establishes nothing: a documented descent does. That is the work the tree below is for.

06

On the word “sept”

Where sept lists come from

Most published sept lists date from the nineteenth century, when clan societies and tartan manufacturers drew up lists linking as many surnames as possible to a clan, so that anyone without a clan name could feel entitled to a tartan.11 The first publication to pair septs and dependents with their clans was Frank Adam's, in 1896.8 A list is a commercial artefact as much as a genealogical one.

Invented tradition

The wider apparatus is no older. Clan tartans as we know them are largely a nineteenth-century creation, and even Walter Scott, who did more than anyone to stage the tartan revival at George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822, doubted their authenticity.9 Scholarship continues to weigh how much of this is Romantic invention and how much genuine survival.10

What that means for this page

None of this makes the connections false. It means sept membership is an association recognised by a clan society, not a proven line of descent, and the two should not be dressed as one. Every source below is tagged with what kind of source it is, so a charter date and a castle legend do not look alike. The family tree carries the descent that can actually be documented.
